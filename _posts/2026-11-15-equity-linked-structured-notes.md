If you've ever been offered something like *"a 5-year note on the S&P 500 with 100% principal protection"* or *"9% a year as long as the stock doesn't fall more than 40%"*, you've met an **equity-linked structured note**. They sound exotic, but the idea underneath is simple: **a bond and a few options, wrapped into a single security.**

This post is a beginner-friendly tour. No stochastic calculus, just enough to read a term sheet and ask the right questions.

> Not investment advice. All numbers below are illustrative and rounded to keep the arithmetic easy.

---

## What is an equity-linked structured note?

A structured note is a **debt security issued by a bank** (or another large financial institution). Like a normal bond, you hand over money today and get money back later. The difference is in *how much* you get back: instead of a fixed coupon, the payout is tied to how an **equity underlying** performs — a single stock, a basket of stocks, or an index like the S&P 500, the TSX 60 or the Euro Stoxx 50.

Two facts matter more than any others:

1. **You never own the stocks.** You own a promise from the bank. No voting rights, and usually no dividends.
2. **That promise is only as good as the bank.** Structured notes are typically senior *unsecured* obligations of the issuer. If the issuer fails, the clever payoff doesn't matter.

### The building blocks

Almost every equity-linked note can be taken apart into two pieces:

| Piece | What it does |
|---|---|
| **A zero-coupon bond** | Grows back to (some or all of) your principal by maturity. This is the "safe" part. |
| **One or more options** | Creates the link to the stock market. Options can be *bought* for you (giving upside) or *sold* on your behalf (earning extra income in exchange for downside risk). |

Change the options and you change the product. That's the whole trick, and it's why the market can produce an almost endless variety of shapes from the same two ingredients.

## A short history

- **Mid-1980s — the first index-linked notes.** As listed index options and futures took off, US banks started packaging stock-market exposure into bonds and CDs. One early example often cited is Salomon Brothers' S&P 500-linked notes in 1986. The pitch: *stock market upside, but you get your money back.*
- **1990s — Europe and the menu grows.** European banks, especially in Switzerland, Germany and France, turned structured products into a mainstream retail business. Payoffs multiplied: capped notes, reverse convertibles, notes on baskets.
- **2000s — Asia and yield products.** Short-dated "ELNs" (essentially reverse convertibles) became a staple of private banking in Hong Kong and Singapore, and Korea built a huge retail market for equity-linked securities (ELS).
- **2008 — the credit-risk wake-up call.** When Lehman Brothers collapsed, holders of Lehman-issued notes — including some marketed as *principal protected* — learned that "protection" was a promise from an issuer that no longer existed. Regulators responded with stricter suitability and disclosure rules for complex products.
- **2010s — the low-rate era.** With interest rates near zero, classic capital-protected notes stopped working (more on why below). The market shifted towards **autocallables** and other yield-enhancement notes, which pay attractive coupons by having the investor take on downside risk.
- **2020s — rates back up, and the occasional painful lesson.** Higher rates made protected notes viable again. Meanwhile, sharp drawdowns in specific markets — China's "snowball" products in early 2024, Korean ELS linked to the Hang Seng China Enterprises Index around the same time — reminded investors what barrier risk looks like in practice.

## Why do structured notes exist?

Because they solve a problem for **each** party involved.

### For investors

- **A tailored risk/return shape.** Want stock upside but can't stomach a loss? Happy to give up upside above 20% in exchange for a cushion against the first 15% of losses? A note can be built to match.
- **Income in a low-yield world.** Selling options generates premium. Packaged into a note, that becomes a headline coupon well above what bonds pay.
- **Access and convenience.** Many investors can't (or shouldn't) trade options directly. A note gives them an option-based strategy in a single security they can hold in a normal brokerage or registered account.

### For banks

- **Funding.** A structured note is the bank borrowing money from you. It's another source of medium-term funding alongside deposits and plain bonds.
- **Fee and trading income.** Banks earn a margin when they design and hedge the product (see the next sections).
- **Risk recycling.** Banks' trading desks end up with exposures they would like to offload (for example, being short volatility). Some note structures transfer those exposures to investors who are happy to take them for a price.

### For distributors

Wealth managers, brokers and private banks earn a **selling commission** for placing notes with their clients, and gain a product they can tailor to a client's view or a market theme.

## Who buys them?

Structured notes are overwhelmingly a **wealth-management product**:

- **Retail and mass-affluent investors** through financial advisors, often in the form of principal-protected notes or market-linked GICs and CDs.
- **High-net-worth clients** through private banks, who tend to buy shorter-dated, higher-coupon structures and more bespoke terms.
- **Some institutions**: insurers, family offices and asset managers occasionally use notes to get a specific exposure in a bond-like format.

The typical buyer falls into one of three camps:

| Investor type | What they want | Typical product |
|---|---|---|
| Cautious, wants equity exposure | Upside without losing capital | Principal-protected / growth note |
| Income seeker | A high, regular coupon | Autocallable, reverse convertible |
| Has a specific view | "This index will be flat-to-up for 3 years" | Buffered, capped or digital notes |

## The main families, by example

### 1. Principal-protected (growth) notes

**Design:** zero-coupon bond + **bought** call option.

Say you invest **$1,000** for **5 years**, and 5-year rates are about **4%**:

- The issuer sets aside about **$819** in a zero-coupon bond. Growing at ~4% a year, that's back to $1,000 at maturity. That's your protection.
- That leaves about **$181**. After costs (see "How banks make money"), roughly **$150** is spent on a 5-year call option on the index.
- With typical market conditions, $150 buys enough calls for around **80% participation** in the index's gain.

At maturity:

| Index at maturity | You receive |
|---|---|
| Up 30% | $1,000 + 80% × $300 = **$1,240** |
| Flat | **$1,000** |
| Down 20% | **$1,000** (protected) |

**Why the low-rate era killed these:** at 1% rates, the zero-coupon bond costs about $951, leaving very little for options. Participation collapses, and the note becomes a bond that pays almost nothing. The product's attractiveness rises and falls with interest rates.

### 2. Buffered and capped notes

**Design:** bond + a mix of bought and sold options.

A common shape: *"1× upside capped at +25%, and a 10% buffer."* You get index gains up to 25%. If the index falls by up to 10%, you lose nothing; beyond that, you lose 1-for-1 (index down 30% → you're down 20%). Selling the upside above the cap pays for the buffer.

### 3. Reverse convertibles and barrier notes

**Design:** bond + **sold** put option.

You receive a fixed, attractive coupon — say **8% a year** — because you've effectively sold insurance on the stock. At maturity:

- If the stock is above the **barrier** (say 70% of its starting level), you get your principal back plus coupons.
- If it's below, you take the stock's loss: down 50% → you get $500 (plus the coupons already paid).

This is what most Asian private banks call an "ELN": often short-dated (1–12 months) on a single stock.

### 4. Autocallables

The most popular structure globally over the last decade. A typical 3-year autocallable on a single stock might read:

- **Quarterly observations.** On each observation date, if the stock is **at or above 100%** of its initial level, the note is **called early**: you get your $1,000 back plus the coupon, and it's over.
- **Contingent coupon of 2.25% a quarter (9% a year),** paid on any observation date where the stock is at or above **70%** (the *coupon barrier*). With a **memory** feature, missed coupons are paid later if the stock recovers above the barrier.
- **Principal barrier at 60%,** observed only at maturity. If the stock finishes below 60%, you lose 1-for-1 with the stock.

Three ways it can play out:

| Scenario | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Stock rises above its start in quarter 2 | Called early: $1,000 + 2 coupons ($45). Short and sweet. |
| Stock drifts between 70% and 100% for 3 years | 12 coupons ($270) + $1,000 back. The ideal case. |
| Stock ends at 45% of its start | $450 back, plus whatever coupons were paid along the way. |

The 9% coupon isn't free money: it's mostly the premium from a **down-and-in put** you've sold, plus a bonus for giving the issuer the right to end the deal early when it's going well for you. Make it a **worst-of** basket of three stocks and the coupon goes up again, because the chance that *one* of them crashes is higher.

## How banks make money

Here's the uncomfortable part for buyers: **the note is worth less than you paid for it on day one.** That's not a scandal, it's how the product is built, and in many jurisdictions issuers must disclose it. US prospectuses, for example, show an **estimated value** on the cover, typically a few percent below the issue price.

A rough breakdown of the $1,000 principal-protected note above:

| Component | Approx. | Who keeps it |
|---|---|---|
| Zero-coupon bond | $819 | Invested for you (you're lending to the bank) |
| Call options | $151 | Invested for you |
| Selling commission | $20 | The advisor / distributor |
| Structuring & hedging margin | $10 | The issuing bank |
| **Estimated value** | **$970 (97%)** | |

The bank's revenue comes from several places:

1. **Structuring margin.** The gap between what the bank charges for the embedded options and what it costs to hedge them.
2. **Funding benefit.** The note is the bank borrowing your money for years at a cost that's attractive relative to its other funding options.
3. **Hedging and trading P&L.** The bank doesn't just sit on the risk; its trading desk **delta-hedges** the position (buying and selling the underlying as it moves) and manages volatility and dividend exposure across thousands of notes.
4. **Secondary-market spread.** If you want out before maturity, the issuer is usually the only buyer, and it quotes a bid that includes a spread.

None of this is hidden in the pricing supplement. It's just rarely the first thing anyone reads.

## What drives the terms you're offered

The same product can look great one month and mediocre the next. These are the dials:

| Factor | Higher means… |
|---|---|
| **Interest rates** | Cheaper zero-coupon bond → more budget for options → better protection or participation |
| **Implied volatility** | Options cost more → *bought* options get pricier (lower participation), *sold* options pay more (higher coupons) |
| **Dividend yield** | Investors usually forgo dividends, which makes upside calls cheaper and puts more valuable |
| **Correlation** (worst-of baskets) | Lower correlation → more likely one stock drops → higher coupon, higher risk |
| **Tenor** | Longer maturity → more time for options to pay off, more time for bad things to happen |
| **Issuer credit spread** | Riskier issuer → higher funding rate → richer terms, but more credit risk |

A useful rule of thumb: **an unusually high coupon is a measurement of risk, not generosity.** High volatility, low correlation, a weak issuer or a near barrier are what pay for it.

## Glossary: terms you'll see on a term sheet

Underlying
: The stock, basket or index the note's payout depends on.

Principal / Notional / Denomination
: The face amount of the note, often $1,000 or $100 per unit.

Issue price
: What you pay, usually 100% of principal.

Trade date / Strike date / Initial level
: The date the terms are fixed, and the underlying's closing level that day. All barriers and call levels are expressed as a percentage of this level.

Valuation date / Final level
: The date the payout is determined, and the underlying's closing level on it.

Maturity / Tenor
: When the note ends and how long it runs.

Participation rate
: The share of the underlying's gain you receive. 80% participation on a 30% gain gives 24%.

Cap
: The maximum return the note can pay, however high the underlying goes.

Buffer
: A fixed amount of loss absorbed before you start losing money (e.g. a 10% buffer means the first 10% of decline doesn't hurt you).

Barrier
: A level that switches the payout on or off. Unlike a buffer, once it's breached you usually take the **full** loss from the initial level, not just the part beyond the barrier.

European vs American (continuous) barrier
: A European barrier is checked only at maturity. An American barrier is checked daily, or even intraday, which makes a breach much more likely.

Knock-in / Knock-out
: A knock-in event *activates* a feature (typically your downside exposure). A knock-out event *ends* it (e.g. an autocall).

Autocall / Call level / Observation dates
: The schedule of dates on which the note can end early if the underlying is above the call level.

Contingent coupon / Coupon barrier
: A coupon paid only if the underlying is above the coupon barrier on the observation date.

Memory coupon
: Missed contingent coupons are paid later if the underlying recovers above the coupon barrier.

Worst-of
: On a basket, the payout follows the worst-performing component. Higher coupons, higher risk.

Snowball (雪球)
: The name used in China for autocallables with a knock-in barrier, usually observed daily.

Principal protection
: A promise to return a set percentage of principal at maturity, subject to the issuer's credit. Selling before maturity is *not* protected.

Estimated value / Initial fair value
: The issuer's own valuation of the note on the trade date. The gap to the issue price reflects fees, margin and hedging costs.

Issuer credit risk
: The risk the issuer can't pay. Applies to every structured note, protected or not.

Secondary market / Liquidity
: Notes don't trade on an exchange. The issuer usually makes a market, but isn't always obliged to, and the price can be well below what you'd expect.

Term sheet / Pricing supplement / Prospectus
: The indicative summary, the final terms, and the full legal documentation, in that order of length.

Delta hedging
: How the issuer neutralizes its exposure to the underlying, by trading the underlying (or futures) as the price moves.

Implied volatility
: The market's expectation of how much the underlying will move, backed out from option prices. The single biggest driver of how generous a note looks.

## Risks to keep in mind

- **Credit risk:** the issuer must survive to maturity.
- **Barrier risk:** losses can arrive suddenly once a barrier is breached, and are often far larger than the coupons earned.
- **Liquidity risk:** getting out early is possible but rarely cheap.
- **Opportunity cost:** caps and forgone dividends can leave you well behind simply holding the index in a strong market.
- **Call risk:** autocallables tend to end early exactly when things are going well, and keep running when they aren't.
- **Complexity:** if you can't sketch the payoff on a napkin, it's hard to judge whether the coupon is fair compensation.

## Questions to ask before buying

1. **Who is the issuer, and how comfortable am I with its credit for the full term?**
2. **What is the estimated value versus the issue price?**
3. **What happens in a bad scenario?** Ask to see the payoff if the underlying falls 30%, 50% or 70%.
4. **Is the barrier observed at maturity only, or daily?**
5. **What would I earn by simply holding the underlying (with dividends) or a bond of the same tenor?**
6. **Can I sell before maturity, and at roughly what cost?**

---

## Wrapping up

Strip away the marketing and every equity-linked structured note is **a bond plus options, issued by a bank.** The bond determines how much of your money comes back; the options decide what you're exposed to; and the gap between the issue price and the estimated value is what everyone in the chain gets paid.

Understood that way, structured notes are neither magic nor a trap. They're a way to buy a very specific risk/return shape, at a price. The job of the investor is to know which shape they're buying and to check that the price is fair.
